<p>I handed four AI agents a written plan and went to bed.&nbsp; In the morning one of them had<br>refused to do the job.<br><br>Not broken.&nbsp; Not confused.&nbsp; It had read the plan, tried to build it, and left a note<br>explaining that what I asked for was impossible.&nbsp; The plan said you could tell whether an<br>address is in Canada or the United States by how far north it is.<br><br>Windsor, Ontario is south of Detroit, Michigan.<br><br>This episode walks through what four agents found overnight with nobody watching, working<br>on a Dynamics 365 Field Service tool that tells a repair crew whether it is safe to drive<br>into a storm.&nbsp; Three of the four findings were the kind that gets somebody hurt: a forecast<br>that returned a confident all clear on hours nobody had looked at, a rule that compared<br>miles per hour against degrees Fahrenheit and threw away a real wind warning, and an alert<br>that argued with itself in front of the one person who had to act on it.<br><br>Then the part that matters more than any of it.&nbsp; The next day, with 169 tests passing, the<br>software was quietly pulling weather for the wrong city.&nbsp; Not one check caught it.&nbsp; I found<br>it in four seconds by asking the real weather service what it thought.<br><br>A test fixture is captured from a request.&nbsp; It proves your parsing.&nbsp; It cannot prove the<br>request was right.&nbsp; The loop verifies coherence.&nbsp; It cannot verify contact.<br><br>Full transcript, verbatim agent quotes, and the numbers that do not flatter us:<br>https://nukasoft.ai/blog/engineering-notes/minutes-of-a-meeting-that-actually-happened/</p>
